Recovery: The Forgotten Pillar of Training

September 10, 2026 · Training

For years, I've been in the gym when I should have been in bed. I've pushed through fatigue, ignored soreness, and treated recovery as a luxury rather than a necessity. It took a season of injury and plateau before I understood that recovery is where training actually happens.

"Progress doesn't occur in the workout. Progress occurs in the rest between workouts."

Why Recovery Gets Overlooked

It's easy to view recovery as "doing nothing." But the truth is far more nuanced. Recovery is an active process involving:

  • Physiological repair — repairing micro-tears in muscle tissue
  • Neuromuscular recovery — allowing your nervous system to reset
  • Metabolic restoration — replenishing energy stores
  • Adaptation — the body actually changing during rest periods

When I first started coaching, I'd see athletes training 6 days a week, then get injured or burned out within months. The programming was solid. The intensity was appropriate. The recovery? Non-existent.

The Three Pillars of Recovery

1. Sleep: The Foundation

Without adequate sleep, everything else matters less. During deep sleep, the body releases growth hormone, repairs tissue, and consolidates learning from training sessions. I recommend:

  • 7-9 hours of quality sleep per night
  • Consistent bedtime and wake time (even weekends)
  • Dark, cool bedroom environment (60-67°F / 15-19°C)
  • No screens 60 minutes before bed

2. Active Recovery Days

Active recovery isn't "doing nothing" — it's moving at an intensity that promotes blood flow without adding significant stress. For me, this means:

  • Light mobility work — 15-20 minutes of dynamic stretching
  • Low-intensity cardio — 20-30 minutes at 50-60% max heart rate
  • Floating — 20-30 minutes in a sauna or hot tub (160-180°F / 71-82°C)

3. Nutrition & Timing

What you eat after training dramatically impacts recovery. The goals are:

  1. Protein — 20-40g within 2 hours post-workout to trigger muscle protein synthesis
  2. Carabohydrates — replenish glycogen stores (3-5g per kg of body weight)
  3. Fluids & Electrolytes — especially important after intense training
  4. Anti-inflammatory foods — fatty fish, leafy greens, berries

Periodic Deloads

Every 6-8 weeks, I recommend a deload week — a week where training volume drops by 40-60% while maintaining intensity. This allows:

  • Accumulated fatigue to dissipate
  • Progressive overload from the previous cycle to integrate
  • Mental break from training
  • Prevention of overtraining syndrome

During a deload:

  • Maintain 70-80% of normal volume
  • Keep working sets at 85%+ of 1RM
  • Focus on mobility and technique
  • Listen to your body — it's asking for more rest

Signs You're Not Recovering Enough

If you're experiencing any of the following, your recovery is insufficient:

  • Chronic fatigue or persistent exhaustion
  • Increased resting heart rate (10+ bpm above baseline)
  • Continued soreness 48+ hours post-workout
  • Declining performance despite increased volume
  • Restless sleep or difficulty sleeping
  • Increased irritability or mood swings
  • Frequent illness or infections

Practical Application

Here's how I apply this to my athletes:

  1. Track recovery metrics — HRV, sleep quality, training RPE
  2. Adjust weekly — if HRV drops 15%+ from baseline, reduce volume by 20%
  3. Weekly check-in — ask athletes "how did recovery feel this week?"
  4. Protect rest days — no training, no gym time, no intense movement

Bottom Line

Training builds the muscle; recovery grows it. When I was younger, I thought training hard was the goal. Now I know that training hard + recovering well = sustainable progress.

Start tracking your recovery this week. Notice how your body responds to different amounts of rest. You might be surprised at what you discover about your own physiology.
